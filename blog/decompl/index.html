DECOMPL: Decompositional Learning with Attention Pooling for Group Activity Recognition from a Single Volleyball Image

Berker Demirel, Huseyin Ozkan

Sabanci University

paper code cite

On this page

    TL;DR

    Group activity recognition models usually process whole clips, often with optical flow or pose keypoints on top of RGB. DECOMPL drops time: it classifies the activity from one RGB frame plus the players' bounding boxes, combining attention-pooled player features with a branch that only looks at where the players stand. It needs about a tenth of the FLOPs of the next-cheapest method in our comparison, and while doing so we found and corrected systematic labeling flaws in the Volleyball dataset.

    The problem

    Group activity recognition (GAR) asks what a group of people is doing together in a short video clip. In volleyball, a label such as left spike or right pass describes the team, not any single player. Even when the decision hinges on one key player, that player's action only makes sense relative to the configuration and actions of everyone else on the court.

    Most prior work models this jointly in space and time. Methods feed RGB frames through recurrent networks, relation modules, graph networks or transformers, and the strongest ones add optical flow, pose keypoints or ball trajectories. Each of those additions improves accuracy, and each adds computation.

    We asked a simpler question. A player's pose and the spatial arrangement of the players are static in a single image, yet they are known to be highly predictive of the action unfolding over time. So, conditioned on good spatial information, how much does GAR in volleyball actually lose if we drop the temporal dimension altogether?

    Betting on a single frame

    DECOMPL treats each frame independently. During training it uniformly samples a single frame from the clip and makes the group activity decision from that frame's visual and coordinate features. At test time it makes an independent decision for every frame and simply averages them. No frame gets special weight and frames are never considered jointly, so the model does no temporal modeling at all. It also uses no optical flow, keypoints or pose estimates: the inputs are the RGB image and the players' boxes, which the Volleyball benchmark provides.

    Two branches

    Visual branch

    A VGG backbone produces a feature map of the frame, and RoI align pools a feature for every player box, projected to \(D = 128\) dimensions. That gives \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}\) with \(N = 12\) players. To capture team-level structure we sort the players by the x-coordinate of their boxes and split the list into a left team and a right team. Each team is summarized by its own multi-head attention pooling module, giving \(X_l, X_r \in \mathbb{R}^D\), and the frame's visual feature \(X_{\text{visual}}\) is their concatenation.

    Attention pooling

    A scene summary should reflect the important players more than the rest. Max pooling makes a hard, index-wise selection, and mean pooling cannot weigh key players, so the summary ends up with a low signal-to-noise ratio. There is also no natural order for players on a court, so the pooling must be permutation invariant. We adopt the attention-based pooling from multiple instance learning (Ilse et al., 2018), which is trainable, permutation invariant, and assigns each player a weight according to their contribution. For embeddings \(X = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}\),

    $$X_{\text{pooled}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i\, x_i, \qquad a_i = \frac{\exp\{w^\top \tanh(V x_i^\top)\}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp\{w^\top \tanh(V x_j^\top)\}},$$

    where \(w \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 1}\), \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times D}\) and \(L\) is the hidden dimension. The coordinate branch uses this module as is; the visual branch stacks several heads and projects their outputs back to \(D\) dimensions. To our knowledge this was the first use of multi-head MIL attention pooling for GAR.

    Coordinate branch

    The second branch never sees pixels. It takes the box coordinates \(X_b \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 4}\), sorts them from left to right, and forms the pairwise difference vectors between players, \(X_{pd} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N \times 4}\). A convolution with kernel size 4 and stride 4 maps each difference vector to a single number with shared weights, further convolutions extract higher-level structure, and a projection gives location features in \(\mathbb{R}^{N \times D}\). A single attention pooling over all players, without splitting them into teams since only relative positions matter here, yields \(X_{\text{coordinate}} \in \mathbb{R}^D\). Because it is independent of the visual features, this branch runs concurrently with the first one.

    Decomposing the label

    Volleyball labels have a mirror symmetry across the two sides of the net, and DECOMPL uses it to add auxiliary tasks. Besides the side-sensitive group activity (for example left spike), each clip also gets a side label (left or right) and a sideless team activity (pass, set, spike, win), on top of the individual action labels. Individual actions are predicted from the per-player RoI features; side, team activity and group activity are each predicted from both \(X_{\text{visual}}\) and \(X_{\text{coordinate}}\), and the two branches' decisions are fused with learnable weights \(\lambda_s, \lambda_g, \lambda_t\). Without visual information the model cannot tell players apart, and without coordinates it is blind to their relative positions, so both are needed. The network is trained end to end with

    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{individual}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{group}} + \beta\,(\mathcal{L}_{\text{side}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{team}}),$$

    with \(\beta = 1\) for the Volleyball dataset.

    Fixing the Volleyball labels

    Working with the Volleyball dataset (VD), the benchmark most of the GAR literature reports on, we noticed that many clips, especially set and pass, were labeled mostly by the pose of the main player. A clip was a pass if the main player was bumping, no matter where the ball went next. That reduces the task to pose recognition and loses the group concept the label is supposed to capture.

    We re-annotated the dataset with group-level semantics. A spike sends the ball to the other side in a comfortable, attacking position. A ball returned defensively, or a touch by the first player after the opponent's last touch, is a pass. A set forwards the ball to the player who is going to spike it. Win-point and the left/right distinction had no systematic flaw, but we also fixed random errors such as side mismatches.

    After removing 9 clips whose camera angle changes, the refined dataset has 4821 clips, and we changed 497 labels, about 10% of the data. Of those, 381 follow from the group-level labeling scheme and 116 (about 2.4%) correct random errors, 29 of them side mismatches.

    What we found

    On the original VD labels, DECOMPL reaches 93.8% group activity accuracy. That is the second-best result among RGB-only methods, 0.3 points behind the best one, and comparable to most keypoint methods and several methods that combine RGB, optical flow and keypoints. Since the original labels contain errors, we also re-ran the methods with public code on our corrected labels.

    With corrected labels, DECOMPL reaches 95.2%, the best among the RGB-only methods and 0.9 points above DIN. Only COMPOSER, which uses keypoints and ball trajectories, is higher; without the ball tracklets COMPOSER falls to 94.39%. DECOMPL also gains the most from the correction: 1.4 points, compared with 0.7 for DIN and 0.35 for GroupFormer. All methods improve with the corrected labels, which is itself a sign that the original ones were holding the benchmark back.

    On the Collective Activity dataset, a different domain with no team structure, we use a single multi-head attention block instead of one per team. DECOMPL reaches 95.5%, 0.9 points above the best RGB-only result and third overall behind two methods that use keypoints or mixed inputs (paper Table 2). This dataset is harder for a single-frame model: waiting and moving are easy to confuse without motion.

    Cost

    Excluding the backbone and embedding layers, as in prior comparisons, DECOMPL needs 0.031 GFLOPs per forward pass and has 0.65M parameters (paper Table 4). The next-lightest method in FLOPs, DIN, needs 0.311 GFLOPs with 1.305M parameters, and COMPOSER needs 0.777 GFLOPs with 11.102M parameters. Modeling time costs computation and parameters; dropping it cuts FLOPs to about 10% of the nearest competitor.

    The coordinate module alone, which only sees where the players stand, already reaches 73.5% without looking at a single pixel. Removing it costs 0.4 points, removing the auxiliary side and team losses costs 0.5, and replacing attention pooling with mean or max pooling costs 0.5 to 0.6. Two attention heads work best (95.2%, versus 94.8% with one head and 94.7–94.8% with four or eight; paper Table 6).

    Takeaways and limits

    • For volleyball, most of the group activity signal is already in a single frame: who stands where and how the key players are posed. Dropping temporal modeling cost little accuracy and saved most of the computation.
    • Where the players are is informative on its own. A pixel-free branch over box coordinates gets 73.5% and still adds to the visual branch.
    • Benchmarks deserve scrutiny. About 10% of VD labels described the main player's pose rather than the group's activity, and correcting them changed how methods compare.
    • The single-frame bet has limits. Activities defined by motion, such as waiting versus moving in the Collective Activity dataset, are ambiguous in one image. The model also relies on given player boxes, and the team split and auxiliary labels exploit the structure of volleyball specifically.
