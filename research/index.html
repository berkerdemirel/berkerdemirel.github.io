Research

I study the geometry and structure of neural representations: how they are organized, when they collapse, and how they behave under distribution shift. That interest spreads into three closely related areas. Representation learning asks what makes a representation good and how to learn one. Generalization asks when what a model has learned carries over beyond its training distribution, and how to tell when it does not. Generation asks how to learn a data distribution well enough to sample from it, and how to judge the samples.

The three keep running into each other. How far a model generalizes depends on the representations it learns; generated samples are judged in learned feature spaces, and they matter most exactly where generalization is in question.

01Representation learning

A representation maps raw inputs to features that other models build on, usually without knowing in advance which tasks will come. Two desiderata make “good” concrete. The representation should be task-agnostic: the information downstream tasks need should be there. And it should be easily probeable: that information should be readable without much effort, for instance by a linear probe.

Open questions

  • What makes a representation good, and how can we tell without knowing the downstream tasks?
  • How can we learn representations that meet both desiderata, ideally without labels?
  • How does the geometry of a representation, the way information spreads across its directions, shape what can be read from it?

02Generalization

Generalization is about when a model can go beyond its training distribution and when it cannot. The training data never pin down a model's behavior everywhere, so how far it carries depends on assumptions about how training and deployment differ: which factors may change, which stay fixed, and whether a change is visible in the inputs at all. When those assumptions fail, the next best thing is to notice, for classifiers and generative models alike.

Open questions

  • When should we expect a model to generalize, and what does that require of the data and the model?
  • Can we detect when a model fails to generalize?
  • How do models from different learning paradigms, such as classifiers and generative models, react to the same shift?

03Generation

Generative modeling is the effort to sample from a data distribution: unconditionally, learning the distribution of the inputs alone, or conditionally, given a label, attributes, or an experimental condition. The model only ever sees a finite sample, so it has to balance staying close to that sample against generalizing beyond it, and judging how well it did is a problem of its own.

Open questions

  • How can we model an input distribution? Diffusion and latent diffusion models, GANs, and drifting models take very different routes.
  • Can a model generate novel examples, or does it memorize its training data?
  • How can we assess whether a generation is good? Distributional metrics, classifier-based evaluation of conditional samples, and independent, domain-specific evaluators each see a different part of the answer.
